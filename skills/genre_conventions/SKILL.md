# Genre Convention Craft

A technically well-written book that ignores its own genre's reader expectations reads as generic or "off" to a genre reader, even when every sentence is polished. This is about delivering on the promise the premise makes, not about formulaic plotting.

## Identify the genre's core promise, and deliver it

Every genre implicitly promises its reader a specific KIND of satisfaction — a mystery promises a fair, solvable puzzle; a romance promises the central relationship actually resolves (positively, in traditional romance); a thriller promises escalating stakes and a ticking clock; horror promises sustained dread, not just isolated shocks. Before drafting, name the specific promise THIS book's genre/subgenre makes, and check the chapter plan against it — a mystery where the solution depends on information the reader was never shown, or a romance where the leads barely interact, breaks the genre contract even if everything else works.

## Genre-specific pacing shapes

- **Mystery/thriller**: escalating revelations, each one raising the stakes or narrowing the suspect pool — not a flat drip of disconnected clues. The reader should be able to make (and periodically revise) guesses; a mystery that's unsolvable until the reveal isn't fair play.
- **Romance**: the relationship needs visible forward AND backward motion (attraction, then a real obstacle/setback, then progress) — a relationship that only ever moves forward in a straight line has no tension; one that never progresses has no payoff.
- **Horror**: dread needs to build cumulatively — early "normal, but slightly wrong" details that pay off later, not just isolated jump-scare beats with nothing connecting them. Horror that peaks too early has nowhere left to escalate to.
- **Fantasy/sci-fi**: worldbuilding details introduced should matter to the plot eventually (see "Chekhov's world-rule" below) — a magic system or technology explained at length that never affects how a conflict is actually resolved reads as padding.

## Chekhov's world-rule

Any specific rule, limitation, or unusual capability of the world that's given real narrative attention (not just background color) should matter to how the plot resolves — if you establish that magic requires a costly sacrifice, that cost should factor into a real decision later; if you establish a technology has a specific weakness, that weakness should matter to how a conflict is won or lost. Introducing a distinctive rule and never using it reads as wasted setup.

## Reader expectations around POV and tension

- Genre fiction generally expects the reader to know more than at least one character at key moments (dramatic irony) — pure mystery-to-the-reader-too is a valid choice but should be deliberate, not accidental; know which one you're doing for a given scene.
- The ending needs to deliver the SPECIFIC kind of resolution the genre promises (a solved case, a couple together, threat neutralized, dread that finally breaks or doesn't) — an ending that's well-written but resolves a DIFFERENT question than the one the genre setup raised will feel unsatisfying even to a reader who can't articulate why.

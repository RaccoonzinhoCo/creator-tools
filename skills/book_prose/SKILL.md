# Scene Craft

This deliberately does NOT restate sentence/paragraph-level prose rules (dash usage, filter words, dialogue tags, rhetorical crutch patterns) — those are already enforced elsewhere in Creator's story pipeline, in full, on every chapter. This is scene-STRUCTURE craft: the level above individual sentences and below the whole-book plan, which nothing else currently covers.

## Every scene needs a goal and an obstacle

Before writing a scene, know two things: what the point-of-view character is trying to get/do/find out IN THIS SCENE specifically (not their book-long goal — their goal for these few pages), and what's making that hard. A scene where the character wants nothing and nothing resists them is a scene that can be cut without the reader noticing — if you can't state both in one sentence each, the scene needs a reason to exist first.

## Scenes end on a change, not a stop

A scene should end with the situation meaningfully different from how it started — a goal achieved at a cost, a goal failed in a way that creates a new problem, or a decision that closes off one path and forces another. A scene that ends with the character simply having *learned something* and feeling *some way about it*, with no resulting change in their circumstances or choices, tends to read as filler even when every individual sentence in it is well-written.

## Alternate scene rhythm: action/reaction

Across a sequence of scenes, vary between scenes that are primarily ACTION (the character does something, pursues the goal, meets the obstacle head-on) and scenes that are primarily REACTION (the character processes what just happened, weighs a decision, changes their plan). An unbroken run of pure-action scenes reads as exhausting; an unbroken run of pure-reaction scenes reads as static. If the last two scenes were both action, the next one should lean reaction, and vice versa.

## Reaction scenes still need forward motion

A reaction scene is NOT license to stall the plot — it should end with the character making an actual decision that sets up the next action scene, not just feeling their feelings and stopping. "She realized she'd been wrong" is not a scene ending; "She realized she'd been wrong, and decided to go back and tell him herself" is.

## Entrances and exits

Start a scene as close to its actual point as the material allows — skip the walking-in, sitting-down, small-talk runway most first drafts default to, unless that runway itself is doing work (establishing something the reader needs). Leave a scene as soon as its goal is resolved (achieved or failed) — don't keep writing past the moment the scene's actual question is answered just because there's more the characters COULD say to each other.

## Every scene should be told from someone with something at stake

If the point-of-view character has no personal stake in how the scene resolves — they're just present to observe and relay information to the reader — that's a sign the scene should either be reassigned to a character who DOES have stakes in it, or cut and the information delivered some other way (a line of dialogue elsewhere, a detail folded into a scene that already has stakes).
